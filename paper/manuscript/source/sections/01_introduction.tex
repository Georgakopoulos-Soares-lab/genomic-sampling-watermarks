\section{Introduction}

Autoregressive genomic language models create a provenance problem: a generated DNA sequence can be
copied or edited after it leaves the model, while ordinary metadata can simply be stripped.
Sampling-time watermarking may embed a keyed statistical signal without retraining, and a fixed 6-mer
tokenization offers a large nominal alphabet of $4{,}096$ symbols. Whether that alphabet translates
into a usable channel, and whether the resulting signal survives the edits DNA actually undergoes, are
empirical questions about specific released models rather than properties of the idea.

We ask whether an exact-marginal keyed sampler supports standalone verification across two genomic
model families under a declared edit channel and a declared adversary. Our contribution is an
empirical and methodological study, not a new cryptographic primitive.

\subsection*{Contributions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The realized channel, not the nominal one.} We measure the watermark information
  actually available at real model states for three released generation policies:
  $\capacityLow{}$ to $\capacityHigh{}$ bit per base against a construction ceiling of
  $\capacityCeiling{}$. The ceiling is structural, so the $4{,}096$-symbol alphabet is not the
  quantity that governs capacity.

  \item \textbf{A detector calibrated over its whole search.} The verifier reports a maximum over
  $\hypothesesSearched{}$ alignment hypotheses, so its nominal tail is not a $p$-value. Every
  threshold here comes from null trials that repeat the identical search, including the windowed and
  wide-offset variants, and the decision rule matches how those exceedances were counted.

  \item \textbf{Synchronization, not capacity, is the binding limit.} Detection survives
  $\substitutionTolerance{}$ substitutions per base but fails at $\deletionTolerance{}$ deletions per
  base. A declared sliding-window search moves that to $\windowedDeletionTolerance{}$ and then meets a
  second wall that no search can move, because a short enough intact run carries too little signal to
  detect at any placement.

  \item \textbf{A matched comparison that does not favour us.} Implementing inverse-transform and
  exponential sampling on the same corpus, prompts, lengths, control, and detector search shows
  exponential sampling carrying at least $\expSignalRatio{}$ times our per-token signal. We report
  that partition coupling sits at its one-bit ceiling and is therefore the weaker construction on
  high-entropy genomic states.

  \item \textbf{A negative security result under key reuse.} With
  $\forgeryDonorsRequired{}$ outputs under one key and no model, key, or detector access, an attacker
  splices a novel sequence that the verifier accepts at rate $\forgeryDetectionRate{}$. The
  construction authenticates a distribution, not a sequence, and the same argument applies to any
  position-indexed content-blind keyed statistic.

  \item \textbf{An evidence discipline that made the negatives visible.} Every printable number enters
  a ledger through explicit review, every figure and table is generated from that ledger, and results
  that were superseded are recorded with the reason. Six pieces of statistical machinery were replaced
  during this work after a number looked too clean; each replacement is documented rather than
  silently applied.
\end{itemize}

Two findings in this paper are the kind that a study designed to advertise its own construction would
not surface. We report them because a provenance mechanism whose limits are unstated is worse than one
whose limits are known.
