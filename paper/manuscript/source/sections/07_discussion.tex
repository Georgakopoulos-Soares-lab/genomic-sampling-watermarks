\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What the construction gives, and what it does not}

Stated without hedging in either direction. The construction gives distribution-preserving, model-free
provenance detection that a standalone verifier recovers from DNA and a key alone, that is complete
from $\shortestDetectedBases{}$ bases, that survives roughly one base in five being substituted and
survives cropping and reverse complementation, and whose false-positive rate is calibrated over every
alignment the detector actually searches. It does not protect against anyone holding
$\forgeryDonorsRequired{}$ outputs from the same key --- not partially, but at all.

Both halves are results. A paper reporting only the first would be advertising; one reporting only the
second would discard real measurements.

\subsection{Why capacity was never the interesting quantity}

The natural expectation for a $4{,}096$-symbol alphabet is a large channel. Partition maximal coupling
couples one bit per token by construction, so the ceiling is $\capacityCeiling{}$ bit per base
regardless of model entropy, and the measured values sit just beneath it. The consequence is that
improving the model, the prompt, or the temperature cannot widen this channel; only a different
construction can. Exponential sampling is exactly such a construction, and the
$\expSignalRatio{}\times$ gap we measure is that structural difference rather than an implementation
gap.

\subsection{Why synchronization is the real obstacle}

For text, a substitution and an insertion are comparable perturbations. For a fixed-block tokenizer
they are not: an insertion or deletion shifts every subsequent token boundary, so a single edit
corrupts the entire remainder rather than one token. The two-order-of-magnitude gap we measure between
substitution and indel tolerance is a property of fixed-block tokenization, and it will appear in any
watermark that reads fixed 6-mers from a fixed origin.

Two mechanisms are worth separating. The declared phase and key-offset search absorbs tens of bases of
drift for free, because after $k$ deleted bases the correct alignment is phase $k \bmod 6$ at offset
$\lceil k/6 \rceil$; we initially predicted much worse detection because we missed this. A windowed
search then extends the reach further by scoring short intact runs rather than diluting them across a
misaligned whole. What neither can overcome is a run too short to carry signal at all, and that is the
wall the windowed results end at.

\subsection{Where a defence would have to live}

Nothing in the construction binds one position to another, or the sequence to a nonce, a length, or a
payload. So the splice attack is not a bug to patch but a consequence of the statistic's shape.
Candidate defences --- content-binding statistics, a per-sequence nonce carried in the sequence, or a
coding layer over the keyed channel --- all change the construction, and none is built or evaluated
here. We name them as directions, not as fixes, because claiming a fix we have not measured is exactly
the failure this paper is trying to avoid.

The coding layer is now unblocked by our own rule: pseudorandom-code and error-correction work was
gated on measuring the edit channel first, and that measurement exists. The channel it would have to
work over is a substitution channel with generous tolerance and an indel channel with almost none,
which is an unusual and demanding target.

\subsection{Calibration does not travel}

Thresholds calibrated on model-generated nulls exceed their target on natural DNA, at up to
$\naturalDNAExceedance{}$, and unevenly across constructions. Natural sequences are repetitive and
low-complexity in ways model output is not. A deployment would need to calibrate on the sequence
population it will actually see, and reporting a false-positive rate without naming the null population
it was calibrated against is not meaningful. We saw this only because public-DNA trials were reported
separately and never pooled into a threshold.

\subsection{What our instruments cannot see, after we built the ones we specified}

The removal attack changes almost nothing in any composition proxy, which is a statement about the
proxies: the attack permutes whole 6-mers, so the 6-mer multiset is preserved exactly and a suite of
counting statistics cannot respond. The obvious remedy was to build the two order-sensitive measures
our own design named --- reading-frame summaries and an independent-model score --- so we did, and
neither prices the attack either.

The reading-frame failure is worth dwelling on because it is a general trap. Its relative shift is
large, and its direction is absent: the longest reading frame rises about as often as it falls under a
bounded shuffle. On high-entropy DNA the longest frame is a chance extreme-value statistic, and
permuting the sequence re-rolls the draw. A magnitude computed from absolute differences cannot tell
that apart from a destroyed structure, and only a paired test can.

This was the seventh time in this project that an unsigned magnitude looked like an effect and a
properly paired null removed it. The others were a non-strict decision rule, an unsigned drift range,
a bootstrap that ignored within-prompt noise, a sign-flip null that ignored a shared fitted direction,
an interval that ignored the calibrated threshold's uncertainty, and an interval that depended on the
order its nulls were pooled. We record them because the pattern is more useful than any one instance:
when a result looks clean, the resampling scheme is the first thing to doubt.

What follows for the paper is a sharper limitation, not a softer one. Any claim that a rearrangement
attack is expensive would need a measure of long-range order that we do not have, and we do not have
one after implementing the two candidates we named in advance.
