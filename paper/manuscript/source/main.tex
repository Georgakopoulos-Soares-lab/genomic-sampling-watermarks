\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Genomic language models now write DNA that reads like the real thing, and a sequence carries no
record of where it came from. A watermark added while the model writes would let the holder of a
secret key recognise the model's output later, but DNA makes this harder than text. A DNA sequence
arrives with no punctuation: the reader does not know which base the model's output starts at, so it
does not know how to group the bases into the words the model actually wrote, and the sequence may
have been reverse-complemented or pasted inside a longer stretch of DNA.

We add the SynthID tournament watermark to a genomic language model that writes DNA six bases at a
time, and we ask two questions. Does the watermark change what the model writes? And can a verifier
find it without being told where the model's output begins? We measure the first against the model's
own likelihood and a pre-specified panel of sequence statistics, and find no difference we can
detect. For the second, we give the verifier nothing but the DNA and the key, and let it search both
strands, every starting base, and four sequence lengths, charging the full cost of that search to a
single answer for each sequence. It found the watermark in every watermarked sequence we held out,
both unedited and after any one base was substituted, inserted, or deleted, while unmarked sequences
and sequences checked with the wrong key behaved as the test predicts. The margin is wide enough
that paying for the search costs almost nothing, and an inserted or deleted base, which shifts every
downstream word, is absorbed by the search rather than by the statistic. These are statistical
results about provenance. They do not speak to biological function, and they do not measure how hard
a deployed key would be to recover.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A DNA sequence carries no record of its own origin. Once it is copied out of the software that
produced it, nothing in the letters says whether a person designed it, an experiment recovered it,
or a model wrote it. That was a minor problem while generative models for DNA were weak. It is no
longer minor. Models trained on genomes now write coding sequences, regulatory elements, and whole
synthetic loci with enough fluency that their output is difficult to separate from natural sequence
by eye or by simple statistics \cite{nguyen2024evo,wu2025generator,carbon2026}. The same fluency has
made provenance a stated concern for the people who screen and synthesise DNA, who have asked for
records that survive from design software through to the ordered
oligonucleotide \cite{baker2024protein}.

One way to leave such a record is to hide a signal in the sequence while the model writes it, so
that whoever holds a secret key can later check a piece of DNA and get an answer without needing the
model, the prompt, or any log of the generation. This is what watermarking does for text. Carrying
it to DNA is not a matter of changing the alphabet. Text arrives with spaces, so a verifier can
recover the tokens the model produced. A DNA sequence arrives as an unbroken run of letters. A model
that writes six bases at a time leaves a signal that only makes sense once the letters are regrouped
into those same six-base words, and there are six ways to group them. The verifier is usually not
told which is right, because it is usually not told where the model's output begins. The sequence
may also arrive reverse-complemented, or embedded in a longer stretch of natural DNA. Each of those
unknowns has to be searched, and every search costs statistical evidence, so a watermark that is
easy to find when the boundary is known can vanish once it is not.

This paper takes one watermark, one genomic language model, and one verifier, and asks whether that
combination survives the honest version of the problem. We add the SynthID tournament
watermark \cite{dathathri2024scalable} to Carbon-500M \cite{carbon2026}, a model that writes DNA in
six-base tokens, and we ask two questions. First, does adding the watermark change what the model
writes, measured by the model's own likelihood and by a panel of sequence statistics fixed before
any comparison was run? Second, can a verifier that holds only the DNA and the key find the
watermark while paying, in full and in advance, for the search over strand, starting base, and
length?

The answers are that we could not measure any change in what the model writes, and that the verifier
found the watermark in every held-out watermarked sequence, unedited and after any single
substituted, inserted, or deleted base, with unmarked and wrong-key sequences behaving as the test
predicts. The evidence margin turns out to be so large that the price of the search is almost free,
and the search itself repairs the damage an insertion or deletion does to the six-base grouping.

We are careful about what those answers cover. We separate four kinds of statement and never mix
them: identities of the construction, assumptions about the key, statistical measurements, and
sequence statistics that stand in for quality. Nothing here shows that the DNA is biologically
functional, viable, or safe. Nothing here shows that a deployed secret key resists recovery. To keep
the argument readable, Table~\ref{tab:vocabulary} fixes one word and one symbol for each idea, and
the rest of the paper uses only those.

\section{Background}

\subsection{Marks written into DNA before models wrote DNA}

Hiding a message in DNA is an old idea, and it predates generative models by two decades. The first
synthetic bacterial genome carried four watermark sequences inserted at positions chosen not to
disturb the cell, encoding names and an email address, so that the synthetic chromosome could be
told apart from its natural counterpart \cite{gibson2010creation}. Schemes such as DNA-Crypt made
the same idea systematic, encoding an encrypted payload into synonymous codon positions and adding
error correction so that the message survives a few mutations \cite{heider2007dna}. A mark of this
kind can be made to survive biology as well as software: hiding text in the synonymous codon choices
of an open reading frame leaves the encoded protein untouched and ties the message to the frame even
when it is taken out of its context, and one such marked viral gene was recovered with the message
intact after 136 days of replication \cite{liss2012watermarks}.

These are insertions. Something extra is added to a sequence that was going to exist anyway, and the
mark is a foreign object inside it. That is workable when a person controls the design and can pick
a harmless location, and it fails in the setting we care about, because there is no separate design
step: the model writes the sequence, and there is no spare region to place a payload in. It is also
brittle in a different way. An inserted marker is a fixed string, so anyone who learns it can find
it, remove it, or forge it.

\subsection{Watermarking what a language model writes}

Watermarking a language model works differently. Nothing is inserted afterwards; instead the
sampling step itself is nudged, so that the output is drawn from a distribution that a keyed test
can recognise but an outside reader cannot. The first widely used scheme splits the vocabulary at
each step into a pseudorandom green list and red list and pushes probability toward the green list,
then detects the mark by counting green tokens \cite{kirchenbauer2023watermark}. It is simple and
strong, and it moves the output distribution, which shows up as a quality cost when the model is
confident.

A second line of work removes that cost. If the keyed randomness is fresh at every step, the
sampling rule can be arranged so that, averaged over keys, the output distribution is exactly the
model's. Cryptographic constructions make this precise and show that, without the key, watermarked
output is computationally indistinguishable from ordinary output \cite{christ2024undetectable}. A
practical family reaches the same property by mapping a key-derived random sequence into a sample
through inverse-transform or exponential-minimum sampling, and by aligning text against that
sequence at detection time, which also buys robustness to editing and
paraphrase \cite{kuditipudi2024robust}; the exponential-minimum rule is usually credited to an
earlier proposal of Aaronson and Kirchner \cite{aaronson2022watermark}. A second branch of the same
family reaches the property by reweighting the model's distribution directly, rather than by mapping
keyed randomness into a sample, and recovers the model's distribution once the average over keys is
taken \cite{hu2024unbiased,wu2024dipmark}; the construction we use belongs to this branch. A third
design gives up the secret instead: a publicly verifiable signature is embedded by rejection
sampling, so anyone can run the detector, at the cost of needing enough entropy in the output to
carry the signature \cite{fairoze2025publicly}. There is a limit to how far robustness can be pushed: an
attacker who can paraphrase a text at quality has, in general, enough power to remove any watermark
from it, so robustness claims are always claims about a stated edit budget rather than about
attackers in general \cite{zhang2024sand}. That budget is not a formality. Paraphrasers tuned
against a particular scheme evade every watermark they were tested on \cite{diaa2025adaptive},
recursive paraphrasing lowers detection rates at modest cost in quality \cite{sadasivan2025reliably},
and querying a watermarked model reveals enough of its rule to both forge and strip the mark
\cite{jovanovic2024stealing}. Keys are attacked as well as marks: the keyed sequence of the
distortion-free family has been recovered by adaptive prompting \cite{reynolds2025breaking}, and
reusing one key across outputs biases the very distribution that family sets out to preserve
\cite{wu2024collisions}. Some of this is answerable in turn---forged text can be told apart from
genuinely marked text after the fact \cite{gloaguen2025spoofing}---and none of it is measured here,
for reasons we state with the other limits.

\subsection{SynthID}

The construction we use is the binary tournament of SynthID-Text, developed at Google
DeepMind \cite{dathathri2024scalable}. At each generation step, a secret key together with a short
window of recently generated tokens determines a set of hidden binary values, one per candidate
token per layer. The candidates are then run through a tournament: at each layer, competitors are
paired and the one whose hidden value is 1 is preferred, so probability flows toward candidates that
happen to carry 1s. Because the hidden values are redrawn for every new context, the tournament does
not favour any particular token overall; averaged over fresh keyed functions it leaves the model's
next-token distribution unchanged. Detection is a count: recompute the hidden values from the output
alone and ask whether too many of them are 1. SynthID is the backbone of this work because two of
its properties matter more in DNA than they do in text. Its hidden values depend on recent tokens
rather than on absolute position, so the mark travels with a sequence that is moved, cropped, or
pasted somewhere else; and its detector needs only the output and the key, not the model, the
prompt, or the probabilities, which is exactly the situation a verifier of a DNA sequence is in. It
has also been evaluated at scale in a live text deployment, which makes it a reasonable place to
start rather than a construction invented for this paper.

\subsection{Genomic language models, and what DNA changes}

Genomic models have moved quickly from representation learning to
generation \cite{dallatorre2025nucleotide,nguyen2024evo}. Several of the recent generative models,
including the one used here, do not read DNA one base at a time; they tokenise it into
non-overlapping six-base words, which shortens sequences and lets the model reach long genomic
contexts \cite{wu2025generator,carbon2026,generatorv22026}. Recent models in this family also
reconcile that coarse tokenisation with single-base resolution by marginalising the token
distribution down to per-base probabilities, and at least one released checkpoint samples base by
base from those marginals even though its paper describes the marginalisation as a training
objective \cite{generatorv22026,generatorv2checkpoint}. What a model samples is therefore a property
of the code that was run, not of the paper that describes it. That choice is what makes the watermarking problem
different. In text, whitespace and subword vocabularies let a verifier recover the token sequence
the model produced. In DNA the tokens are invisible: the six-base grouping depends entirely on where
counting starts, so a verifier that does not know the boundary must consider all six groupings.
Reverse complementation doubles that, and cropping or embedding a generated stretch in a longer
sequence multiplies it again. A test that reports the best result over all of those attempts without
paying for them is not a test at all, which is why the price of the search is central to what
follows.

Watermarking for DNA models is new. Recent work embeds marks in designed coding sequences through
synonymous codon substitution and propagates them from DNA into the translated protein, which is a
different and complementary goal: it aims at function-preserving marks in coding regions, evaluated
by detection accuracy on the designed sequences \cite{zhang2025securing}. The same reweighting
idea has been carried into protein design, where a keyed mark lets a synthesiser verify locally that
a sequence came from an authorised user without reporting the sequence to a central
database \cite{chen2025protein}. Our question is narrower
and, we think, prior to it. We do not assume the generated region is coding, we do not assume it can
be aligned to anything, and we do not assume the verifier knows where it starts. We take an existing
watermark whose distributional behaviour is already understood, put it inside a genomic model, and
measure what a verifier can honestly conclude from a bare stretch of DNA and a key.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Model and sampling}

All generation used Carbon-500M \cite{carbon2026} at one pinned revision of its released
checkpoint \cite{carbon500m}, a decoder-only genomic language model that tokenises DNA into
non-overlapping six-base words. At each step the model's logits were restricted
to the 4{,}096 canonical DNA tokens and normalised once. Temperature was 1.0 and no top-$k$ or
top-$p$ truncation was applied. The ordinary arm samples from that distribution directly and uses no
key. The marked arm applies the tournament described below and then samples.

\subsection{Writing the mark}

At each step the model gives a probability $p$ to each of 4{,}096 tokens. The key, the four tokens
of context, and each candidate token together fix that candidate's mark bit $g$, which is 0 or 1.
Mark bits are derived with HMAC-SHA-256 over the key, a fixed domain label, the context, and the
candidate token. The tournament formula, the number of layers, the context width, and the
repeated-context rule follow the public reference implementation of
SynthID-Text \cite{synthidtextcode}, at the revision pinned for this study. One layer of the
tournament reweights the whole distribution,
\begin{equation}
p'(x) \;=\; p(x)\,\Bigl(1 + g(x) - \textstyle\sum_y p(y)\, g(y)\Bigr),
\label{eq:layer}
\end{equation}
so candidates whose bit is 1 gain probability and the rest lose it, in proportion to how much of the
probability mass carries a 1. Applying this update once per layer is equivalent to the binary
tournament without ever building its $2^{m}$ leaves. The sampler applies $m = 30$ layers, each with
its own bits, and then draws a token from the result $p_k$. Figure~\ref{fig:method}a shows one
layer.

Two details make the mark readable later. The first four written tokens are sampled ordinarily,
which lets the verifier rebuild every later context from the output alone. When a four-token context
repeats, that step is sampled ordinarily and is left out of the score, following the original
construction; a repetition history of 1{,}024 contexts is kept for this purpose.

Averaged over fresh keyed functions, Equation~\ref{eq:layer} leaves the model's next-token
distribution unchanged; that is an identity of the construction. With one fixed key and one fixed
context it does not. The sampler then draws from $p_k$, a calculated reweighting of $p$, and $p_k$
is the law we tested the sampler against.

\subsection{Reading the mark}

The verifier receives the read, the key, the generation domain label, and settings fixed before any
of these reads existed. It does not receive the prompt, the model, the model's probabilities, the
seed, the strand, the token grouping, or the point where the model's output begins
(Figure~\ref{fig:method}b).

It therefore searches. For each of the four window lengths $L$ that fit the read, it tries the read
as given and its reverse complement, and inside each it tries every starting base. Stepping one base
at a time covers the unknown boundary and all six token groupings at once. Inside a window it
regroups the DNA into tokens from that start, treats the first four tokens as context, recomputes
the mark bits for the rest, and counts how many are 1. That gives $S$ ones out of $n$ scored bits.

Without the right key those bits are modelled as fair coin flips, so the chance probability of one
window is the exact binomial tail
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{win}} \;=\; \Pr\bigl[\mathrm{Binomial}(n, \tfrac{1}{2}) \geq S\bigr].
\end{equation}
The verifier scores $M = 2\sum_L (N - L + 1)$ windows, which is 16{,}136 for a 3{,}456-base read,
and reports one answer for the whole read:
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{read}} \;=\; \min\bigl(1,\; M \cdot \min_{\text{windows}} P_{\mathrm{win}}\bigr),
\qquad
\text{mark found} \iff P_{\mathrm{read}} \leq \alpha .
\end{equation}
Multiplying by $M$ pays for every window that was tried, including overlapping ones. Nothing about
the threshold was fitted to these reads: $\alpha = 0.01$ is set in advance and the correction
follows from the number of windows. Equivalently, a read is called only if some window reaches
strength $\log_{10}(M/\alpha) = 6.21$. Window lengths are 384, 768, 1{,}536 and 3{,}072 bases; a
window whose length does not fit the read is not searched, and a read shorter than every pre-specified
length is refused rather than silently rescored. Reverse-complement coordinates are mapped back onto
the supplied read, and log-probabilities are retained throughout so that strong evidence is not lost
to floating-point underflow.

\subsection{Prompts and generation}

The 256 prompts are 384-base forward-strand windows drawn from 16 accession-versioned RefSeq
chromosome records across eight model organisms. Windows were chosen by hashing a fixed label, the
accession, and a segment index, before any generation existed. Human, patient, controlled-access,
organelle, plasmid, and ambiguous-base spans were excluded. No prompt was added, moved, or removed
after any model was run.

Each prompt produced two marked and two ordinary continuations of 512 tokens (3{,}072 bases). The
two draws use different replay seeds in both arms, and the two marked draws use two different keys.
Both keys are published with this study so the runs can be reproduced; a deployment would use an
independently generated secret key that never leaves the generating system.

\subsection{Checking the sampler}

For each prompt we fixed the model at token index 64 of its ordinary draw-zero trajectory and drew
5{,}000 samples per arm from that one state. Counts were compared with the exact intended law by a
Monte Carlo goodness-of-fit test with 999 replicates. The marked arm was compared against $p_k$, not
against $p$; comparing it against $p$ would test a claim the construction does not make at a fixed
key. Eight further states were run with a deliberately broken sampler as a negative control.

\subsection{Sequence measures}

The model's score is the teacher-forced negative log-likelihood per written token under the same
model and token restriction used to generate, with the prompt supplied as context and excluded from
the loss. Thirteen further measures were fixed before analysis: GC content, purine content, CpG
content, base, dinucleotide and trinucleotide entropy, distinct 6-mer fraction, longest and mean
single-base run, Jensen--Shannon drift from the prompt at $k = 1, 2, 3$, and perplexity. All 14 form
one multiple-testing family under Benjamini--Hochberg correction.

\subsection{Edits and statistics}

Each edited read carries exactly one substituted, inserted, or deleted base inside the written part,
at a position and with a replacement base drawn by a fixed random rule that does not consult the
verifier. Prompts are the independent unit; the two draws from a prompt are paired repetitions.
Uncertainty on the sequence measures comes from an equal-weight bootstrap over prompts with 20{,}000
replicates and a paired sign-flip test with 100{,}000 replicates. Detection intervals are exact
two-sided binomial intervals at 95\% over the 192 held-out prompts, and a prompt counts as positive
if either of its draws was called. Window-level probabilities are exact binomial tails, and the
read-level answer multiplies the smallest of them by the number of windows searched, so every
$P_{\mathrm{read}}$ we report already covers each strand, start, and length tried. No smallest
window probability is reported as a read-level result anywhere in this paper.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Sampler fidelity: the sampler draws from the law it calculates}

Before asking what the mark costs, we checked that the sampler implements the construction. Thirteen
of 256 marked states fell below the nominal 0.05 level against 12.8 expected by chance, and the
ordinary arm gave 12. Neither arm produced a rejection after correction, while all eight states run
with a deliberately broken sampler were rejected. The implementation therefore behaves as intended
at the 256 states and 5{,}000 draws we tested, which is a statement about those states rather than a
proof for every state the model can reach.

\subsection{Sequence quality: the mark leaves no measurable trace}

For every prompt we generated two marked and two ordinary continuations of 3{,}072 bases under the
same conditions, giving 512 matched pairs from 256 prompts.

The model's own score was 7.53386 nats per 6-mer for ordinary output and 7.54238 for marked output.
The paired difference is $+0.00852$ nats per 6-mer, with a 95\% interval from $-0.04426$ to
$+0.05973$ and $P = 0.75$ (Figure~\ref{fig:quality}a); as a standardized effect that is 0.0201. The
thirteen further measures, covering base composition, $k$-mer entropy, distinct 6-mers, single-base
runs, CpG content, and drift away from the prompt's composition, behave the same way. Every one of
the 14 intervals contains zero, the largest effect in absolute value is 0.084 standard deviations,
none survives correction for multiple testing, and the smallest adjusted $P$-value is 0.85
(Figure~\ref{fig:quality}b).

We therefore found no measurable loss, which is weaker than saying there is none: the interval on
the model score still allows a change of roughly $\pm 0.05$ nats per 6-mer, and these are model
scores and sequence statistics rather than measures of biological function.

\subsection{Recall and false-positive rate: the mark is found in every marked read}

We split the prompts once, before looking at any detection result: 64 prompts were used to choose
between two ways of summarizing a window's score, and 192 were held out. The threshold was not
fitted on either side of that split. For the held-out prompts we glued the 384-base prompt in front
of each continuation, withheld the boundary, and gave the verifier the resulting 3{,}456-base read.
Each read was checked in three ways---marked with the right key, ordinary, and marked but checked
with the other draw's key---and in four conditions: unedited, and after exactly one substituted,
inserted, or deleted base.

The verifier found the mark in 384 of 384 marked reads in all four conditions
(Table~\ref{tab:detection}), a recall of 100\%. One of 384 ordinary reads passed, a false-positive
rate of 0.26\%, and no read checked with a wrong key passed, in any condition. We report recall and
the two false-positive rates separately rather than a pooled accuracy, because how many marked,
ordinary, and wrong-key reads a cohort contains is a design choice, and a pooled figure would move
with that choice rather than with the verifier.

The separation is not marginal. On unedited reads the weakest marked read reached window strength
124.8, while the strongest ordinary read reached 6.60 and the strongest wrong-key read reached 5.69,
against a threshold of 6.21 (Figure~\ref{fig:detection}a). The single ordinary read that passed sits
just above the line, which is what a correctly calibrated test at $\alpha = 0.01$ should
occasionally produce.

Because the two draws from a prompt are not independent, we count prompts. All 192 prompts had both
marked draws found, giving an exact 95\% interval of 98.10--100\%. One prompt of 192 produced an
ordinary positive, giving 0.013--2.868\%, and no prompt produced a wrong-key positive, giving an
upper bound of 1.903\% (Figure~\ref{fig:detection}b). The four ordinary positives in
Table~\ref{tab:detection} are one prompt and draw counted four times, not four events: its edit
happened to fall outside the window that carried its evidence, so the same read passed in all four
conditions.

\subsection{Edit robustness: one edited base does not hide the mark}

A substitution changes the mark bits only near itself, and the evidence barely moves: the median
window strength falls from 790.6 on unedited reads to 774.7 (Figure~\ref{fig:edits}a). An insertion
or a deletion is different in kind. It shifts every downstream base into a different token grouping,
so the long window that carried the evidence before the edit is now half correct and half
scrambled.

The search absorbs this by itself. On unedited reads the full 3{,}072-base window carried the
strongest evidence in 383 of 384 reads. After an insertion it did so in only 203, with 181 reads
best explained by a shorter window; after a deletion, 232 and 152 (Figure~\ref{fig:edits}b). A
window that starts after the edit and stops before the read runs out recovers the correct grouping,
and the verifier finds it because it tried every start. The median strength falls to 431.5 for an
insertion and 443.7 for a deletion, and the weakest read in any condition still reached 82.2 against
a threshold of 6.21. Every marked read was still found.

\section{Discussion}

The verifier in these experiments works without the things a detector is usually assumed to have. It
is not told the prompt, the model, the strand, the token grouping, or where the model's output
begins, and it still found every marked read in this corpus. The cost of that freedom is explicit
rather than hidden: the answer is multiplied by the number of windows tried, so a larger search
weakens every claim it might support. What makes the approach practical is that the mark, spread
over 3{,}072 written bases, has evidence far in excess of what the threshold demands, so paying for
16{,}136 windows costs a factor that the signal does not notice. This is the main thing the numbers
say, and it is also the main thing that will not transfer automatically: a short generated stretch
would spend most of its evidence on the search alone, and a longer read would pay a larger
correction for the same signal, so the length at which position-independent detection stops working
is a real quantity that this study does not measure.

The behaviour under insertion and deletion is worth separating from ordinary robustness. A
substitution is a local event and the count barely moves. An insertion or deletion is not local at
all in a model that writes six bases at a time, because it slides every downstream base into a
different word and destroys the grouping that the long window depended on. The evidence does drop by
roughly half, but detection does not fail, and the reason is visible in which window wins: after an
indel the strongest evidence usually comes from a shorter window that starts past the edit, where
the grouping has recovered. The search that made the problem expensive is the same mechanism that
repairs it. That is a property of searching every start, not a general robustness guarantee, and it
says nothing about a read carrying many edits. It is also worth being clear that no watermark
survives an adversary who can rewrite a sequence at quality; robustness claims of this kind are
always claims about a stated edit budget \cite{zhang2024sand}, and ours is one substitution,
insertion, or deletion applied by a rule that never looks at the verifier.

Coding theory has treated this asymmetry as the harder case for a long time, because an insertion or
a deletion destroys the index rather than one symbol, and the standard remedy is to restore the index
explicitly: indexing a stream with a synchronisation string turns synchronisation errors back into
ordinary substitution errors, so a conventional code can finish the job
\cite{haeupler2017synchronization}. The cryptographic counterpart is a pseudorandom code, whose
codewords stay indistinguishable from random while remaining decodable under substitutions and
deletions, which is the property a watermark on an editable channel wants
\cite{christ2024prc}. Our verifier buys the same synchronisation by brute force instead. That needs
no change to the sampler and no redundancy in the sequence, and it pays for itself in the size of the
search rather than in the construction.

Complementary work marks designed coding sequences through synonymous substitution and carries the
mark into the translated protein \cite{zhang2025securing}; that approach assumes a
coding region and a design step, and it answers a question about function preservation that ours
does not. The two are naturally combined, and the combination is a better description of what a
provenance system for synthetic DNA would eventually need than either alone.

\section{Limitations}

The scope is one model, one watermark, and one verifier, and it is one implementation on one corpus
whose confirmatory replication is still outstanding: everything above is a single execution of this
sampler and this detector, and none of it has yet been reproduced independently of that run.
Carbon-500M writes six-base tokens, and so do several other current genomic models
\cite{wu2025generator}, which is what makes the grouping problem general rather than particular; but
models that read DNA one base at a time face a different version of it, and models with much longer
context would change the arithmetic of the search.

The false-positive behaviour is consistent with the 1\% target but does not establish it: one
ordinary prompt of 192 passed and no wrong-key prompt did, which is what a correctly calibrated test
should produce, yet 192 independent prompts cannot resolve a rate near 1\%, since the interval
around the ordinary result runs to 2.87\% and the wrong-key upper bound is 1.90\%; pinning the rate
down needs more independent prompts, not a different verifier.

Neither is any of this a security result, because the keys used here are published so that the study
can be reproduced, and a published key cannot test whether a deployed secret key could be recovered
from many observed outputs; key secrecy is an assumption we make rather than a finding, as is the
standard modelling assumption that the mark bits behave like fair coin flips to anyone without the
key. In text, keys of the distortion-free family have been recovered by adaptive prompting
\cite{reynolds2025breaking} and watermark rules reverse-engineered by querying the watermarked model
\cite{jovanovic2024stealing}; whether either transfers to a 4{,}096-token genomic vocabulary is a
question this study does not ask, and the attacker assumed throughout neither holds the key nor sees
the verifier's score.

And none of it is a biological result: the model's score and the 14 sequence measures are statistics
about letters, they do not measure whether the DNA folds, expresses, replicates, or is safe, and a
watermark that leaves those statistics unchanged has not thereby been shown to leave function
unchanged. That last gap is the one most worth closing, and closing it needs assays rather than more
sequence statistics.

\section{Conclusion}

Adding the SynthID tournament watermark to a genomic language model cost nothing we could measure in
the model's own likelihood or in 14 pre-specified sequence measures. A verifier holding only the DNA
and the key found the watermark in every one of 384 held-out marked reads, unedited and after any
single substituted, inserted, or deleted base, while paying in full for a search over both strands,
every starting base, and four window lengths. Unmarked and wrong-key reads behaved as the test
predicts, though 192 prompts are too few to place a tight bound near a 1\% false-positive rate. The
result that matters beyond this corpus is structural: when a generated stretch is long enough, the
statistical price of not knowing where it begins is small, and the same exhaustive search that makes
the problem hard is what repairs the frame shift an inserted or deleted base causes.
