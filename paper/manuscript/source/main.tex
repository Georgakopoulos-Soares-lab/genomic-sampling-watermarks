\textbf{Abstract.}
Genomic language models can generate synthetic DNA sequences, yet the resulting sequences contain
no inherent record of their origin. Generation-time watermarking could supply such provenance.
In DNA, however, the unknown location of the generated region, six-mer token boundaries, and strand
orientation complicate detection, particularly when generated sequence is embedded within a longer read.

We integrate the SynthID tournament watermark into Carbon-500M and GENERator-v2 1.2B---two
genomic language models that process and generate non-overlapping six-mer tokens under the
evaluated sampling policy. We evaluate whether a verifier can recover the mark using only DNA, a
key, and fixed public settings. The verifier applies one read-level correction across both
strands, every start position, and four window lengths. In each model, it detected all 384
held-out marked reads when unedited and after each tested single-base substitution, insertion,
or deletion. Ordinary and wrong-key controls yielded no more than one positive result for each
model, control family, and condition. Neither model showed a statistically detectable difference
in model likelihood or the pre-specified sequence measures after multiple-testing correction. In
both models, window-level results are consistent with preserved or recovered six-mer token
alignment after insertions and deletions. These findings support statistical provenance
detection within the tested corpus and portability of the implementation across the two
evaluated models. They do not establish biological function, secret-key security, or robustness
beyond the evaluated non-adaptive single-base edits. \end{minipage} \vspace{1.2em} \end{center}

\section{Introduction}

Genomic language models increasingly support the generation of coding sequences, regulatory
elements, genome-scale constructs, and functionally constrained DNA designs
\cite{nguyen2024evo,wu2025generator,carbon2026,generatorv22026,king2026bacteriophages}. A
nucleotide sequence, however, carries no inherent record of whether it originated in a
biological sample, a computational design process, or a generative model. That provenance can be
lost when a sequence is separated from the logs and metadata maintained by its generating system.
Provenance mechanisms that remain associated with a sequence throughout design and synthesis are therefore of
increasing interest \cite{baker2024protein}.

Watermarking provides one approach to this problem by embedding a statistical signal during
generation \cite{kirchenbauer2023watermark}. A verifier holding the corresponding key can later
test for that signal without access to the model, prompt, or generation logs. In DNA, this becomes
a synchronization problem. Models that generate non-overlapping six-mer tokens do not encode their
token boundaries in the emitted sequence, leaving six possible token phases. Reverse complementation
changes token identities and their order, so verification must consider both strand orientations.
Surrounding sequence can also obscure the generation boundaries: where the generated region begins
and ends within the read.
The verifier must search these alternatives while controlling the false-positive probability
of the final read-level decision.

Here, we integrate the SynthID tournament watermark \cite{dathathri2024scalable} into Carbon-500M
\cite{carbon2026} and GENERator-v2 1.2B \cite{generatorv22026}. Under the evaluated policy, these
models share non-overlapping six-mer tokenization and direct sampling from canonical tokens, but
differ in size, training corpus, and tokenizer construction.
We ask whether the sampler follows its intended fixed-key distribution, whether watermarking
changes the pre-specified model and sequence proxies, and whether a verifier can recover the
mark without knowing the generation boundary, strand, or token phase. The detector searches both
strands, every start position, and four window lengths, then applies one correction covering the
complete search.

In both models, the verifier detected every held-out marked read under the tested unedited and
single-base edit conditions. The quality-proxy comparisons showed no statistically detectable
difference after correction, and ordinary and wrong-key control counts were compatible with the
declared false-positive target. In both models, the window-level results are consistent with the
search selecting regions with preserved or recovered six-mer token alignment after insertions and
deletions. These findings establish statistical detection within the evaluated corpus, not biological
function, secret-key security, or equivalence between the models.
Distribution preservation denotes an expectation over fresh keyed functions; at a fixed key and
context, sampling follows the explicitly reweighted distribution.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Watermarking designed DNA}

DNA watermarking predates its application to generative models. The first synthetic bacterial
genome included watermark sequences encoding identifying information to distinguish the synthetic
chromosome from its natural counterpart \cite{gibson2010creation}. DNA-Crypt formalized message
embedding through synonymous codon choices and error correction \cite{heider2007dna}. Related
approaches embed information within an open reading frame while preserving the encoded amino acid
sequence, with experimental evidence that embedded messages can persist during viral replication
\cite{liss2012watermarks}.

These approaches rely on explicit sequence design, through either payload insertion or
modification of selected nucleotides. They require suitable embedding locations and
consideration of the biological consequences of those changes. Generation-time watermarking
addresses a complementary setting: the mark is introduced through the model's sampling
distribution as the sequence is generated, without requiring a separate payload region. A fixed,
recognizable marker also differs from a distributed keyed signal in its susceptibility to
targeted removal or copying.

\subsection{Watermarking language-model output}

Language-model watermarking modifies token sampling to create a statistically detectable pattern.
Some methods bias a pseudorandom subset of tokens and detect their excess frequency
\cite{kirchenbauer2023watermark}; others preserve the original distribution in expectation over
fresh keyed randomness \cite{christ2024undetectable,kuditipudi2024robust,aaronson2022watermark,
hu2024unbiased,wu2024dipmark}. SynthID belongs to the latter class. We study its application to DNA,
rather than proposing a new watermark construction.

Robustness depends on the attacker's capabilities and the permitted edits. Prior work identifies
evasion, key-recovery, and forgery risks under stronger adaptive capabilities
\cite{zhang2024sand,diaa2025adaptive,
sadasivan2025reliably,jovanovic2024stealing,reynolds2025breaking}. The present study tests
statistical detection after one non-adaptive base edit and does not evaluate those attacks.

Text-domain watermarks that target robustness to editing treat synchronization as a local
problem. Kuditipudi et al.\ align a candidate text to a key sequence under an edit distance
\cite{kuditipudi2024robust}; Kirchenbauer et al.\ search all contiguous spans for the strongest
window and calibrate to a fixed false-positive rate under that search
\cite{kirchenbauer2024reliability}; and Golowich and Moitra obtain robustness to a constant
fraction of insertions, substitutions, and deletions by indexing a pseudorandom code
\cite{golowich2024edit}, borrowing from the synchronization codes of coding theory
\cite{haeupler2017synchronization}. The first two rely on an insertion or deletion in text
perturbing tokenization near the edit while leaving distant tokens largely intact; the third
does not, but it assumes the ability to index the stream, which we cannot do without altering
the nucleotide sequence itself. Non-overlapping $k$-mer genomic tokenization removes that
locality. A single inserted or deleted base shifts the reading
frame, so every downstream token changes identity, and the keyed context that determines each
mark bit changes with it: the damage is not local but total from the edit onward. An
edit-distance alignment against a key sequence does not recover this, because there is no
surviving token sequence to align; what survives is a contiguous region in one of six phases.
This is why verification here is posed as a search over strands, phases, start positions, and
window lengths rather than as an alignment problem, and why the correction over that search is
part of the method rather than an afterthought.

The distinction is sharper than a difference of degree. Context-free constructions remove key
desynchronization entirely, fixing the vocabulary partition once so that a positional shift
cannot change which tokens carry the mark \cite{zhao2024provable}, which in that paper's own
analysis buys twice the edit tolerance of the context-dependent soft watermark rather than the
guarantee itself. It would still fail here, because the failure
under a frameshift is not desynchronization of the key but destruction of token identity: the
downstream six-mers become different vocabulary items, and a different item carries an
independent mark bit. Conversely, watermarks defined above the tokenizer, on sentence embeddings
rather than tokens, are tokenization-independent by construction \cite{hou2024semstamp} --- but
they require an encoder at verification and a natural segmentation into units, and we have
neither a model at verification time nor an intrinsic segmentation of genomic sequence.

These constructions can be compared along four properties that bear directly on the present
setting: whether verification needs the generating model or its probabilities; whether it needs
an alignment, a known boundary, or a known strand; what edit model is claimed; and whether
sampling is distorted. Biased-logit schemes \cite{kirchenbauer2023watermark} distort the output
distribution by construction and are typically verified on a sequence whose boundaries are
known. Distribution-preserving schemes
\cite{christ2024undetectable,kuditipudi2024robust,hu2024unbiased,wu2024dipmark,dathathri2024scalable}
leave the distribution unchanged in expectation over fresh keyed randomness; SynthID belongs to
this class in its non-distortionary configuration. Among them, edit robustness is pursued by different means:
Kuditipudi et al.\ recover synchronization by alignment to a key sequence
\cite{kuditipudi2024robust}, whereas DiPmark certifies a radius of token modifications under a
context-hashed green-list statistic \cite{wu2024dipmark}. Watermarking of designed
or generated biological sequence takes a different route again: synonymous codon substitution
assumes a coding region and an identifiable reading frame \cite{zhang2025securing}, and keyed
watermarking of protein generation operates on the generated protein rather than on the DNA
encoding it \cite{chen2025protein}. The construction evaluated here needs no model access, no alignment, no known boundary,
and no coding assumption, and it preserves the sampling distribution in the same expectation
sense. We did not run a same-cohort empirical comparison against any of these alternatives:
doing so would require implementing a second watermark construction, which is outside this
study's scope, and the resulting comparison would confound the construction with the verifier
that is our contribution. We therefore compare properties, not measured performance.

\subsection{SynthID}

We use the binary tournament construction of SynthID-Text \cite{dathathri2024scalable}. At each
generation step, a secret key and a short context of recently generated tokens determine binary
mark bits for each candidate token and tournament layer. Each layer favors candidates with a
mark bit of 1. Averaged over fresh keyed functions, the tournament preserves the model's
next-token distribution; at a fixed key and context, it samples from a reweighted distribution.
Detection reconstructs the mark bits from the output and tests for an excess of 1s.

Two properties motivate this construction for genomic sequences. First, the mark bits depend on
local token context rather than absolute position, allowing detection to use contexts retained
when a generated region is relocated or cropped. Second, the detector requires only the output,
key, and public settings; it does not need the generating model or its probabilities.
SynthID-Text has also been evaluated in a large-scale text deployment
\cite{dathathri2024scalable}.

Keying on a hash of recent tokens rather than on absolute position is a standard feature of
autoregressive text watermarks, including the red/green-list construction of Kirchenbauer et al.
\cite{kirchenbauer2023watermark}. We adopt SynthID not because local context is unique to it but
because it combines that property with tournament sampling, whose distribution-preservation
identity holds in expectation over fresh keyed functions. Local context is what makes
verification possible at an unknown location; distribution preservation is what makes the
generated sequence a draw from the model's own law in expectation over fresh keyed functions,
with sampling at the fixed key following the calculated reweighted law.

\subsection{Genomic tokenization and alignment}

Several generative genomic models, including both evaluated here, tokenize DNA into
non-overlapping six-base tokens to reduce token-sequence length
\cite{wu2025generator,carbon2026,generatorv22026}. The watermark sampler operates on the
distribution used to sample those tokens. We therefore use each model's direct canonical
4{,}096-token distribution; for GENERator, this excludes the released base-by-base sampling path
\cite{generatorv2checkpoint}. This shared policy definition makes the sampling comparison
meaningful; a shared vocabulary alone would not suffice.

The phase problem is specific to multi-mer tokenization. Single-nucleotide models place one
token per base \cite{nguyen2024evo,nguyen2023hyenadna}, so an insertion or deletion shifts token
positions without changing token identities, and a verifier faces an offset rather than a frame.
Multi-mer tokenization is nevertheless widely used, because it shortens sequences by the token
width and lets a model cover more bases within a fixed context: the Nucleotide Transformer
selects six-mers as a trade-off between sequence length and embedding size
\cite{dallatorre2025nucleotide}, and GENERator reports that a $k$-mer tokenizer outperforms
byte-pair encoding for next-token pretraining \cite{wu2025generator}. Both models evaluated here
use non-overlapping six-mers. The cost is that six distinct phases must be considered at
verification time, and that an indel changes the identity of every downstream token rather than
only its position. This cost is recognized in model design: Caduceus adopts character-level
tokenization precisely because minor changes to an input sequence can otherwise produce
drastically different tokenizations \cite{schiff2024caduceus}, while GENERator instead absorbs
the problem during training by randomizing the tokenization start offset of each sample
\cite{wu2025generator}. Neither remedy is available to a verifier that receives only DNA, which
must recover the phase from the sequence itself.

Six-mer tokenization also creates the study's central synchronization problem: token boundaries
are not represented in the emitted nucleotide sequence. Here, token phase is the nucleotide
offset at which the sequence is partitioned into consecutive six-mers; it does not refer to
alignment to a reference genome. A verifier must therefore consider all six phases, both strand
orientations, and candidate locations and lengths of the generated region. Selecting the
strongest window without correcting for that search would overstate the evidence. Our verifier
therefore uses an observed read, a key, and public settings without assuming a coding region,
reference alignment, or known generation boundary. This setting is complementary to genomic
watermarking through synonymous codon substitution \cite{zhang2025securing} and keyed
watermarking of protein generation \cite{chen2025protein}.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Shared model policy and sampling}

  Generation used
  Carbon-500M \cite{carbon2026,carbon500m} and GENERator-v2-eukaryote-1.2b-base
  \cite{generatorv22026,generatorv2checkpoint}, two decoder-only genomic language models with
  non-overlapping six-base tokens. At every step, we restricted logits to the 4{,}096 canonical DNA
  tokens and normalized once. For GENERator, this specifies its direct canonical token distribution,
  not the base-by-base path used by its released code by default. Temperature was 1.0, with no top-$k$
  or top-$p$ truncation.

This policy defines the distribution $p$ used by both experimental arms: the ordinary arm
samples directly from $p$ without a key, whereas the marked arm applies the tournament below
before sampling. We evaluated the models separately using the same generation,
sampler-validation, sequence-quality, and detection procedures and report results for each
model.

Generation ran on GPU hardware. Model scoring ran on CPU for Carbon and on GPU for GENERator, and
detection ran on x86-64 CPUs for both.

\subsection{Watermark embedding}

At each generation step, the model assigns probability $p$ to each of 4{,}096 tokens. The key,
the preceding four tokens of context, and the candidate token determine its binary mark bit $g$.
Mark bits are derived with HMAC-SHA-256 over the key, a fixed domain label, the context, and the
candidate token. The tournament formula, the number of layers, the context width, and the
repeated-context rule follow the public reference implementation of
SynthID-Text \cite{synthidtextcode}. One tournament layer reweights the distribution as
\begin{equation}
p'(x) \;=\; p(x)\,\Bigl(1 + g(x) - \textstyle\sum_y p(y)\, g(y)\Bigr),
\label{eq:layer}
\end{equation}
so that candidates with a mark bit of 1 gain probability according to the total probability mass
assigned to such candidates. Iterating this update is equivalent to evaluating the binary
tournament without explicitly constructing its $2^{m}$ leaves. The sampler applies $m = 30$
layers, each with its own mark bits, and samples a token from the resulting distribution $p_k$
(Figure~\ref{fig:method}a).

The first four generated tokens are sampled ordinarily so that subsequent watermark contexts can
be reconstructed from the continuation alone. A repeated four-token context is also sampled
ordinarily and excluded from the detector score, following the original construction. The
sampler maintains a history of 1{,}024 contexts to detect repetitions.

Averaged over fresh keyed functions, Equation~\ref{eq:layer} leaves the model's next-token
distribution unchanged. This identity does not imply equality at a fixed key and context, where
the sampler follows the explicitly reweighted distribution $p_k$. Sampler fidelity was therefore
evaluated against $p_k$.

\subsection{Position-independent detection}

The verifier receives the read, the key, the generation domain label, and fixed public detector
settings. It does not receive the prompt, the model, the model's probabilities,
the seed, the strand orientation, the token alignment, or the generation boundary
(Figure~\ref{fig:method}b).

For each of the four window lengths $L$ that fit within the read, the verifier evaluates every
starting base on the supplied strand and its reverse complement. This search covers candidate
generation boundaries and all six token alignments. Within each window, DNA is partitioned into
tokens relative to the window start. The first four tokens provide context; the verifier
reconstructs mark bits for the remaining eligible tokens and obtains $S$ ones among $n$ scored bits.

Under the null assumption that mark bits behave as independent fair binary values, the
window-level $P$-value is the exact binomial tail
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{win}} \;=\; \Pr\bigl[\mathrm{Binomial}(n, \tfrac{1}{2}) \geq S\bigr].
\end{equation}
The verifier scores $M = 2\sum_L (N - L + 1)$ windows, which is 16{,}136 for a 3{,}456-base read,
and reports a single read-level result:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
P_{\mathrm{read}} &\;=\; \min\bigl(1,\; M \cdot \min_{\text{windows}} P_{\mathrm{win}}\bigr),\\
\text{mark found} &\iff P_{\mathrm{read}} \leq \alpha .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The Bonferroni correction by $M$ accounts for all searched windows, including overlapping windows,
without requiring independence between their tests. The threshold uses the pre-specified
$\alpha = 0.01$ and is not fitted to the evaluated reads. Equivalently, a read is positive only if
at least one window reaches
strength $\log_{10}(M/\alpha) = 6.21$. The window lengths are 384, 768, 1{,}536, and 3{,}072 bases. The verifier skips window lengths that exceed the read length and does not score reads shorter than every pre-specified window length. Reverse-complement coordinates are mapped back onto the supplied read, and
log-probabilities are retained throughout to avoid floating-point underflow.

The $M$ searched windows are far from independent: two windows of the same length whose starts
differ by a multiple of six bases share a token phase and all but a few of their tokens, so
their binomial statistics are strongly positively correlated. Bonferroni correction bounds the
family-wise error rate without assuming independence, which is why it is used here, but in a
correlated family it is conservative: the effective number of independent hypotheses is smaller
than $M$, so the realized false-positive rate is below the declared target and the required
per-window evidence is higher than a correlation-aware correction would demand. Our recall is
therefore attained under a threshold stricter than necessary, and the reported control counts
should be read as an upper bound on the operational false-positive rate rather than an estimate
of it.

The search geometry also imposes a floor on what any single window can prove. Each scored token
contributes one mark bit per tournament layer, so a window carries $30$ bits per scored token. At
the shortest evaluated length, 384 bases is 64 tokens, of which the first four supply
unwatermarked context, leaving at most 60 scored tokens and at most 1{,}800 mark bits, fewer
where a four-token context repeats. With all 60 scored, the corrected threshold requires at least
1{,}004 of the 1{,}800 bits to be positive, or about 56\%. The floor on scored tokens themselves
is far lower: a window of only 21 mark bits reaches the threshold when all 21 are positive, so a
single scored token, which supplies 30 bits, would already suffice. The binding constraint is therefore not
the mark-bit count but the shortest window the verifier evaluates, which is 384 bases. Detection
requires a contiguous region whose token alignment is preserved across a window of at least that
length, which is the precise sense in which the method depends on recoverable synchronization
rather than on the absence of edits.

The search cost is dominated by keyed hashing rather than by the window enumeration. For a read
of $N$ bases there are twelve token frames, two orientations by six phases, and the mark bits
of every token position in a frame are computed once in a single pass. Window statistics then
follow from prefix sums over those per-token bit counts, so each window of a given length is
evaluated in constant time; where a frame contains repeated four-token contexts, the
first-occurrence rule is enforced with Fenwick trees and each window costs $O(\log N)$ instead.
The work is therefore $O(N)$ keyed hash evaluations and $O(N \log N)$ additional time with
$O(N)$ space per read, with $M$ growing linearly in $N$ for a fixed window set.

\subsection{Prompts and generation}
\label{sec:prompts}

Both models used the same cohort of 256 prompts: 384-base forward-strand windows drawn from 16
accession-versioned RefSeq chromosome records across eight model organisms. Before generation,
windows were chosen by hashing a fixed label, the accession, and a segment index. Human,
patient, controlled-access, organelle, plasmid, and ambiguous-base spans were excluded. No
prompt was added, moved, or removed after any model was run.

For each model, every prompt produced two marked and two ordinary continuations of 512 tokens
(3{,}072 bases), yielding 512 marked and 512 ordinary continuations per model. The two draws
used different replay seeds in both arms, and the two marked draws used different keys. Both
keys are published for reproducibility. Deployment would require independently generated secret
keys shared only with authorized generators and verifiers.

\subsection{Sampler validation}

For each model and prompt, we fixed the model state at token index 64 of its ordinary draw-zero
trajectory and drew 5{,}000 samples per arm from that state. We compared the counts with the exact intended
distribution using the likelihood-ratio ($G$) statistic, referred to a parametric Monte Carlo
null of 999 replicates resampled from the declared law rather than to its asymptotic $\chi^2$
distribution. At 4{,}096 categories and 5{,}000 draws per state the expected count per category
is about 1.2, so the asymptotic reference is unreliable; the Monte Carlo reference is exact
under the declared law by construction. The
marked arm was compared against $p_k$, not against $p$, because the distribution-preservation
identity does not hold at a fixed key and context. Eight additional states per model used a
deliberately incorrect sampler as a negative control.

\subsection{Sequence measures}

The model score is the teacher-forced negative log-likelihood per generated token, using the
same model and token restriction as generation. The prompt is supplied as context and excluded
from the loss. Each model scores its own outputs, so absolute scores are not used for
comparisons between models. Thirteen additional measures were specified before analysis: GC
content, purine content, CpG content, base, dinucleotide, and trinucleotide entropy, distinct
6-mer fraction, longest and mean single-base run, Jensen--Shannon drift from the prompt at $k =
1, 2, 3$, and perplexity. All 14 form one multiple-testing family under Benjamini--Hochberg
correction.

\subsection{Edits and statistics}

Each edited read contains exactly one substituted, inserted, or deleted base within the
continuation. A fixed random rule selects the position and, where applicable, the replacement or
inserted base without consulting the verifier. Prompts are the independent unit; the two draws
from a prompt are paired repetitions. Uncertainty in the sequence measures is assessed using an
equal-weight bootstrap over prompts with 20{,}000 replicates and a paired sign-flip test with
100{,}000 replicates. Detection rates are reported with exact two-sided 95\% binomial intervals
over the 192 held-out prompts. For control families, a prompt is positive if either draw is
positive; marked-output recall is also assessed by requiring both draws to be detected. All
reported read-level $P$-values include the correction over every searched strand, starting base,
and window length.

\section{Results}

We first report sampler fidelity and sequence-quality comparisons, then evaluate
position-independent detection and its response to single-base edits. The first two checks
provide context for interpreting detection, the study's primary practical outcome. Unless
otherwise stated, results are reported separately for Carbon and GENERator using the same
cohort, read construction, and decision rule.

\subsection{Sampler fidelity}

Each sampler agreed with its intended distribution at the tested model states. Among 256 marked
states per model, tests for 13 Carbon states and 5 GENERator states yielded nominal $P$-values
below 0.05, compared with 12.8 expected by chance; the corresponding ordinary-arm counts were 12
and 14. The 256 per-state tests form one family per arm. We report Benjamini--Hochberg
correction at $\alpha = 0.05$ as the primary correction. We also record Bonferroni correction at
$\alpha/256 = 1.95 \times 10^{-4}$, but note that 999 Monte Carlo replicates bound the attainable
$P$-value below at $10^{-3}$, so no state can reject at that level and the Bonferroni result
carries no information at this family size. Neither the marked nor the ordinary arm produced a
rejection under either correction in either model, whereas the deliberately incorrect sampler, whose eight control
states form their own family, was rejected at all eight in each model. This supports sampler fidelity at the tested states and at the resolution provided
by 5{,}000 draws per state; it is not a proof of correctness at every possible model state.

\subsection{Sequence-quality proxies}

For every model and prompt, we generated two marked and two ordinary continuations of 3{,}072
bases, giving 512 matched pairs from 256 prompts per model. Marked and ordinary output showed no
statistically detectable difference in either model across the pre-specified 14-measure family
(Figure~\ref{fig:quality}). All intervals contained zero, and no comparison was significant
after multiple-testing correction; the smallest adjusted $P$-value was 0.85 for Carbon and 0.73
for GENERator.

The paired marked-minus-ordinary difference in model score was $+0.00852$ nats per token in
Carbon (95\% interval $-0.04426$ to $+0.05973$; $P = 0.75$) and $-0.00564$ in GENERator (95\%
interval $-0.05614$ to $+0.04378$; $P = 0.83$). The largest absolute standardized effects were
0.084 standard deviations in Carbon, on the longest single-base run, and 0.111 in GENERator, on
the mean single-base run; every interval included zero and no measure survived correction in
either model. These results indicate no measurable quality loss under
the evaluated proxies, but they do not establish equivalence, relative biological quality
between models, or biological function.

\subsection{Recall and false-positive rate}

The prompt split was specified before examining detection results: 64 prompts were reserved for
selection between two window-score summaries, and 192 were held out for evaluation. Neither
subset was used to fit the detection threshold. The two candidate summaries were the released
SynthID mean score over scored tokens and the upstream default weighted-mean score. They were
compared on the 64 calibration prompts alone, at continuation lengths of 384 and 3{,}072 bases
scored under known token alignment, by the separation between the marked and ordinary arms in
units of the ordinary-arm standard deviation. The mean score separated the arms at least as
well at both lengths and was selected. Evaluation prompts were never scored during this
selection and no signal-matched weights were fitted. For each model, the 384-base prompt was
prepended to each held-out continuation, producing a 3{,}456-base read whose generation boundary
was withheld from the verifier. Three detection families were evaluated: marked reads with the
correct key, ordinary reads, and marked reads with the other draw's key. Each family was
evaluated unedited and after exactly one substitution, insertion, or deletion. The search is
determined by the read and window lengths and is identical across models. Unedited reads require
$M = 16{,}136$ windows and a threshold of 6.21; the window count is recomputed for the read
length after an edit.

In both models, the verifier detected all 384 marked reads in every condition, including each
tested single-base edit (Table~\ref{tab:detection}). Among the controls, Carbon produced one
ordinary positive and no wrong-key positives; GENERator produced no ordinary positives and one
wrong-key positive in the unedited, substitution, and insertion conditions. Each positive cell
represents 1 of 384 reads (0.26\%). Recall and the two control rates are reported separately
because a pooled accuracy would depend on the prevalence of marked and control reads in an
application.

Figure~\ref{fig:detection} shows the separation between marked reads and controls relative to
the corrected detection threshold. In Carbon, the weakest unedited marked read had strength
124.8, compared with the 6.21 corrected threshold; in GENERator, the corresponding value was
19.6. Both clear the threshold with wide margin---the weakest GENERator read by 13.4 orders of
magnitude in $P_{\mathrm{win}}$ and the weakest Carbon read by 118.6---and we do not attribute
the difference in margin to a specific cause. Candidate explanations include the number of tokens scored per
read after repeated-context exclusion and differences in predictive entropy between the models;
distinguishing them requires per-token analysis we leave to future work. The detection decision
is unaffected: every marked read in both models cleared the corrected threshold. Control
strengths were concentrated near or below the threshold, with the few positive controls only
slightly above it. At the prompt level, the exact 95\% interval for all marked
prompts detected was 98.10--100\% in both models. For control families, the upper bound with
zero positive prompts was 1.903\%, and the interval for one positive prompt was 0.013--2.868\%;
these data are compatible with, but do not establish, an operational false-positive rate below
1\%.

The GENERator aligned-detector diagnostic provides a separate check of the analytic null under
known token alignment. Across the four evaluated lengths, 2, 6, 6, and 4 ordinary-output prompts
were positive, versus approximately five expected at each length ($P = 0.25$--$0.87$). This result
is compatible with the analytic null, but it does not replace the full-search correction used for
the position-independent decision.

\subsection{Detection after single-base edits}

The edit experiment evaluates whether detection persists and how the search responds to
disrupted token alignment. Every marked read remained detectable after the tested substitution,
insertion, and deletion in both models (Table~\ref{tab:detection}). A substitution perturbs
token content and nearby watermark contexts while preserving downstream alignment; an insertion
or deletion shifts the grouping of every subsequent token in a window spanning the edit.

The distribution of strongest-window lengths shows a consistent pattern across models
(Figure~\ref{fig:edits}). For unedited reads, the full 3{,}072-base window carried the strongest
evidence in 383 of 384 Carbon reads and 381 of 384 GENERator reads. After insertion, those
counts fell to 203 and 200; after deletion, they were 232 and 227. This shift toward shorter
windows is consistent with the search selecting regions whose alignment is preserved or
recovered. It does not imply a general guarantee against more extensive or adversarial
modification.

\section{Discussion}

We evaluated the SynthID tournament watermark in Carbon-500M and GENERator-v2 1.2B using a
shared prompt cohort and position-independent detector. For both models, the samplers agreed
with their intended distributions at the tested states, the declared quality proxies showed no
statistically detectable loss, and every held-out marked read was detected in the unedited and
tested single-base edit conditions. The methodological contribution is a shared watermarking and
detection procedure that accounts for uncertainty in strand orientation, start position, and
window length at the read level.

Detection remained effective after correction for alignment and location uncertainty. The
verifier searches both strands, every starting base, and four window lengths, and includes every
tested window in the read-level correction. In both models, the window-level results show that
the accumulated signal exceeded the corrected threshold. This margin should not be assumed for
shorter continuations or longer surrounding reads: shorter generated regions provide fewer mark
bits, while longer reads increase the number of candidate windows. The minimum detectable
continuation length under these conditions remains to be established.

Applying the same sampler and verifier to two models supports portability across the evaluated
model implementations. The detector depends on the observed read and public settings, so its
statistical procedure is unchanged between Carbon and GENERator. Their agreement in detection
outcomes and the absence of measurable quality loss provide evidence beyond a single model
application. However, the models share the same prompt cohort and token length, limiting
extrapolation to other corpora and tokenization schemes. This comparison also does not establish
relative biological quality or equivalence between the models.

The strongest-window results help explain how alignment search supports detection after
insertions and deletions. A substitution preserves downstream token boundaries, whereas an
insertion or deletion changes the alignment of every subsequent token within a window spanning
the edit. The increased selection of shorter windows is consistent with the verifier selecting
regions whose alignment remains usable in both Carbon-500M and GENERator-v2 1.2B. Exhaustive
search over starting bases also permits recovery of the original alignment downstream of an
edit. The strongest window does not identify the exact generation boundaries. This
interpretation accounts for the observed single-edit results but provides no general guarantee
against more extensive modification. Theoretical work on watermark removal further emphasizes
that robustness depends on the permitted adversarial capabilities \cite{zhang2024sand}. Here,
each read received exactly one randomly specified edit, without access to the verifier's score.

The alignment problem is related to synchronization errors in coding theory. Synchronization
strings explicitly encode indexing information to support recovery from insertions and deletions
\cite{haeupler2017synchronization}. Pseudorandom codes combine computational
indistinguishability with decodability under specified error models, motivating their use in
watermarking \cite{christ2024prc}. A longer-standing alternative treats the offset as a latent variable rather than a hypothesis to
be enumerated: watermark codes for insertion-deletion channels---where the watermark is a known pseudorandom
carrier sequence rather than a provenance mark---recover synchronization with a hidden Markov
model whose state is the accumulated drift, integrating over offsets instead of testing them
separately \cite{davey2001reliable}. That formulation presumes control of the
encoding, which a sampling watermark does not have. Our verifier instead addresses alignment
uncertainty through exhaustive search, without adding an explicit indexing code to the
sequence. The cost of this
search is the number of windows evaluated and the associated multiple-testing correction. This
analogy concerns the alignment problem; it does not imply the error-correction guarantees of
those coding constructions.

Watermarking designed coding sequences through synonymous substitution, including approaches that
preserve the mark in translated proteins \cite{zhang2025securing}, addresses a complementary
objective. Such methods assume a coding region and permit explicit sequence modification, whereas
our evaluation concerns detection in generated DNA without those assumptions. Combining these
approaches may support broader provenance workflows, but their compatibility and biological
consequences would require separate evaluation.

This study evaluates one watermark construction against its matched ordinary-sampling control,
and reports no same-cohort empirical comparison against an alternative construction such as a
biased-logit scheme adapted to six-mer tokens. We state that absence as a limitation: the
property comparison in Section~\ref{sec:background} distinguishes the constructions on model
access, alignment requirements, edit model, and sampling distortion, but it does not substitute
for measured performance on a shared cohort.

Future work should evaluate the trade-off between watermark strength, detection power, and
sequence-level distortion across different tournament depths in both Carbon-500M and
GENERator-v2 1.2B.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

This evaluation covers two models, one watermark construction, and one verifier; it does not
provide a general proof of watermark robustness, biological safety, or secret-key security. The
evaluation used one execution per model on a shared prompt cohort. Both models use six-base
tokens; single-base or variable-length tokenization would present different alignment
requirements. The shared cohort limits extrapolation to other genomic sources, and longer reads
would increase the search correction. Generation ran on GPUs and detection on x86-64 CPUs,
without independent replication across computing platforms. For Carbon, the retained protocol
document differs from the hash recorded in the detection result. This unresolved provenance
discrepancy limits verification of that execution. We were unable to determine which revision of
the protocol document the recorded hash corresponds to. The parameters that document governs are
the cohort identity, the prompt split rule, the four window lengths, both orientations, the
declared false-positive target, and the tournament and context settings; all of these are
independently recorded in the result artifact's own configuration and command fields and agree
with the reported analysis, but the document-level chain for this execution cannot be closed,
and we report it as an unresolved provenance gap rather than a resolved one.

The robustness evaluation covers exactly one non-adaptive substitution, insertion, or deletion
per read, an edit rate near 0.03\%, and this is a declared scope boundary rather than an
incidental limit. The design makes the consequence of raising that rate explicit. Detection
requires a contiguous region whose six-mer alignment survives across one of the evaluated
windows, the shortest of which spans 384 bases. Independent indels arriving at rate
$r$ leave expected clean stretches of about $1/r$ bases, so at $r = 0.1\%$ the expected clean
stretch is near 1{,}000 bases and detection should often remain possible, whereas at an illustrative $r = 1\%$ the expected clean stretch falls near 100 bases, below our shortest window, and the verifier should fail. Substitutions are
milder than indels in this respect: a substitution corrupts the tokens overlapping it without
shifting the frame, whereas a single indel shifts every downstream token. We therefore claim
provenance detection only in the single-edit regime, and we identify the search geometry, not
the watermark strength, as the binding constraint at higher rates. This clean-stretch argument
is an order-of-magnitude consequence of the search geometry, not a measurement. Establishing
the empirical breakdown point requires a shorter-window search with a correspondingly larger
correction, which is outside this study's declared threat model and is the most useful direction
for the next one.

The observed control counts are compatible with the 1\% target but do not establish an
operational false-positive rate below it. With 192 independent prompts, the 95\% interval
reaches 2.87\% for one positive prompt; the upper bound for zero positives is 1.90\%. We did not
scale the cohort for this version; precise estimation near a 1\% target requires roughly an
order of magnitude more independent prompts, which we identify as required future work rather
than a resolved question.

Finally, the experiments use published keys for reproducibility and therefore do not evaluate
secrecy, resistance to key recovery, or adversarial removal in deployment. The analytic null
assumes independent fair mark bits without the correct key. Whether attacks reported for text
watermarking \cite{reynolds2025breaking,jovanovic2024stealing} transfer to this genomic setting
is untested; the assumed attacker does not hold the key or observe the verifier's score.
Likewise, model likelihood and sequence statistics are biological proxies, not measures of
expression, replication, function, viability, or safety. Assessing biological consequences would
require experimental assays.

Between sequence statistics and wet-lab assays lies a class of standardized in-silico
evaluations---supervised downstream task suites and zero-shot variant-effect benchmarks
\cite{marin2024bend,liu2024genbench,patel2024darteval}---that we do not use here. Our quality
claim is deliberately confined to the 14 declared model-likelihood and sequence summaries, and it
establishes only that watermarked and ordinary outputs are statistically indistinguishable under
those measures. These suites do not transfer directly to the present setting. They score a
model's reading of annotated DNA rather than the biological plausibility of what it writes, and
their metrics are in almost every case defined by held-out labels tied to genome coordinates that
a de novo generation does not carry. The narrow exceptions do not close the gap: a motif-based
protocol needs no coordinates, but presence of the motif is then itself the ground truth. Evaluating watermarked generations against them would therefore require either a
paired design, in which a generated sequence is scored against a deliberately altered copy of
itself, or experimental assay. Constructing such an evaluation is the natural next step before
any claim about biological consequence, and we do not make one here.

\section{Conclusion}

The SynthID tournament watermark produced no statistically detectable loss in the evaluated
model-likelihood and sequence-quality proxies for Carbon-500M or GENERator-v2 1.2B. With
read-level correction over both strands, every starting base, and four window lengths, the
position-independent verifier detected all 384 held-out marked reads per model, unedited and
after each tested single-base substitution, insertion, or deletion. Control counts were
compatible with the declared false-positive target, but 192 independent prompts limit precision
near 1\%. In both models, strongest-window distributions are consistent with the search
selecting regions with preserved or recovered token alignment after insertions and deletions.
These findings support statistical provenance detection within the evaluated scope. Broader
conclusions require independent replication, wider sequence coverage, and biological validation.
